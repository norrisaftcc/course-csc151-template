# AI Teammate Policy — CSC 151 Java Programming I

**Status:** Template policy. Instructors must confirm it against their institution's academic integrity policy before the term starts.

---

## The short version

You may use an AI coding assistant in this course. You must be able to coach it, and you must be able to explain what it produced.

---

## Why this course allows it

Programmers use these tools. A course that bans them teaches a workflow the student will abandon on their first job. A course that ignores them lets students submit work they cannot read.

This course takes the third path. The AI is a teammate. You are the coach. A coach who cannot evaluate a player's work is not coaching.

---

## The five rules

### 1. The AI may be the player. You must be the coach.

The player writes code. The coach states the goal, guards the gate, asks questions, and requests evidence. You may hand the player role to an AI. You may not hand over the coach role.

### 2. The AI may never open the execute gate.

Only a person says "Agreed." The gate is an agreement between you and your human coach — your instructor or your partner. An AI cannot confirm your prediction on your behalf, and its approval is not a handshake.

This rule is not administrative. The gate exists so that a shared understanding is confirmed before execution. An AI agreeing with you confirms nothing about your understanding.

### 3. Predict before you run, every time.

The prediction is yours. Write it before you ask the AI for anything, or before you run what it wrote. If the output surprises you, that surprise is the most useful information in the lesson. Do not let the tool take it from you.

### 4. Disclose what the AI produced.

Record it on your [coach/player record](../templates/coach-player-record.md). Name what the AI wrote, what you changed, and what you kept. Disclosure is not a penalty. Undisclosed use is.

### 5. You must be able to explain any line you submit.

Your coach may point at any line of your submission and ask one diagnostic question. If you cannot answer, the work is not yours yet. Take it back to the smaller task.

---

## The standard, stated plainly

| You did the work when you can | You have not finished when you |
|-------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| State the goal in your own words | Restate the AI's summary |
| Predict the output before running | Run first and read the output as your prediction |
| Point to the line that produces a given result | Describe the program only in general terms |
| Explain why a change breaks the program | Say "the AI wrote that part" |
| Name one input that would make it fail | Assert that it works |

---

## On the bus, or off the bus

> **"You are either on the bus or off the bus."**

**Literal meaning:** the bus is the course process — predict, complete the handshake, execute, report evidence, explain the result. The phrase comes from American team sports, where the team bus leaves on schedule and everyone who is travelling is aboard. You do not need to know anything about sports to take this course.

The bus is the process, not the tooling.

- Using an AI teammate and completing every step: **on the bus.**
- Writing every line by hand and skipping the prediction: **off the bus.**

The tool you use is not the question. Whether you can explain the result is the question.

---

## How this interacts with the execute gate

The gate is unchanged. All four parts still apply, and the coach still opens it.

| Gate item | What you state | What the AI may do |
|-----------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Goal** | "The program must…" | Nothing. The goal is yours to state. |
| **Constraints** | "I may change… I may not change…" | Nothing. Constraints come from the task. |
| **Prediction** | "I expect the output to be…" | Nothing. The prediction must be yours, written first. |
| **Success check** | "We know it works when…" | May suggest test cases after you state your own. |

After the gate opens, the AI may help you write, trace, or repair code. It may not report your evidence and it may not write your explanation.

---

## Notes for instructors

- **Ask one diagnostic question at submission.** This is the whole enforcement mechanism, and it takes about thirty seconds. A student who used the tool well answers easily.
- **Do not ask students to prove they did not use a tool.** That standard cannot be met and it punishes honest students. Ask what the work does instead.
- **Treat detection software as unreliable evidence.** Base academic integrity decisions on what a student can explain, not on a classifier score.
- **Say the policy out loud in week 1.** Students who are unsure whether help is allowed will either avoid a useful tool or hide their use of it. Both outcomes are worse than a clear rule.
- **Adapt the disclosure standard** to your institution's policy before the term starts. See open question 5 in the [CLO/MLO map](clo-mlo-map.md).
